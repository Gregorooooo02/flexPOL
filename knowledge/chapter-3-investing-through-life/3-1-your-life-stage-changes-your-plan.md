# 3.1 Your Life Stage Changes Your Plan

**Chapter:** 3 — Investing Through Life
**Goal of this subchapter:** the learner understands that a good plan changes over a lifetime, as horizon, income, responsibilities and risk capacity change.

---

## Lesson

### One plan doesn't last a lifetime

A plan that's perfect at 25 may be wrong at 60. Not because investing rules change, but because **you** change: your goals, income, family and time horizon.

### What shifts with each life stage

| Factor | Early career | Mid-life | Near retirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Time horizon to retirement** | Very long (30–40 years) | Medium (15–30 years) | Short (under 10–15 years) |
| **Income** | Often lower, growing | Often at its highest | Stable, then ending |
| **Responsibilities** | Fewer | Family, home, several goals | Fewer dependents, health costs |
| **Savings built up** | Small | Growing | At its largest |
| **Risk capacity** | Usually higher | Medium | Usually lower |

### Human capital

When you're young, your biggest asset is often your **future earning power**, called *human capital*. You may have little money saved, but decades of income ahead. That income can absorb investment losses and rebuild savings.

Near retirement, the money you've saved has to replace your salary. A large loss is much harder to recover from.

### Not only age

Age is a starting point, not a rule. Other things matter just as much:

- **Job stability:** a stable job allows more risk than an unpredictable income.
- **Dependents:** children or family who rely on you call for more safety.
- **Health** and other personal circumstances.
- **Several goals at once:** each goal can have its own horizon and its own allocation.

### Key takeaways

- A good plan adapts as your life changes.
- Risk capacity is usually higher when young and lower near retirement.
- Future earning power (human capital) helps young people absorb losses.
- Age matters, but so do job stability, dependents and personal goals.

## Control questions

### Q1. Why do younger investors usually have a higher risk capacity for retirement savings?
- A) They know more about investing.
- B) They have a long horizon and future income to recover from losses. ✅
- C) Young people's investments can't lose value.
- D) They pay no fees.

**Explanation:** time and future income give room to recover.

### Q2. What is "human capital"?
- A) Money in a savings account
- B) The value of your future earning power ✅
- C) The number of employees at a company
- D) A type of fund

**Explanation:** your ability to earn income is an asset, often the biggest one when young.

### Q3. Two 35-year-olds: one has a stable job and no dependents, the other has unpredictable income and three children. Who likely has the higher risk capacity?
- A) The one with the stable job and no dependents ✅
- B) The one with three children
- C) They're the same, because they're the same age.
- D) Neither can invest.

**Explanation:** age is only one factor; stability and responsibilities matter too.

### Q4. Why is a large loss more dangerous near retirement?
- A) Fees are higher for older people.
- B) There's less time and less future income to recover. ✅
- C) Markets fall more for older investors.
- D) It isn't more dangerous.

**Explanation:** with a short horizon, savings need to be used soon, and there is no salary to rebuild them.

### Q5 (reflection). Think of a fictional person at your current life stage. What is one thing in their life that should shape their investment plan?

**What a good answer mentions:** a concrete factor (a horizon, a goal, job stability, dependents) and how it affects the amount of risk.
