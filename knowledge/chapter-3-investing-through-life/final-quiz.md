### Q1. Besides age, which factor matters most for how much risk someone can afford?
- A) Their favorite type of company
- B) Which bank they use
- C) Job stability and whether others depend on their income ✅
- D) What their friends invest in

**Explanation:** age is a starting point, but stability and responsibilities shape risk capacity too. *(3.1)*

### Q2. Why is "human capital" an advantage for young investors?
- A) Decades of future income can help absorb losses and rebuild savings. ✅
- B) It removes the need for an emergency fund.
- C) It lowers investment fees.
- D) It guarantees investment returns.

**Explanation:** future earning power gives young people room to recover from setbacks. *(3.1)*

### Q3. Ana starts investing 100 coins a month at 25, and Ben starts the same amount at 35. Both stop at 65. Why does Ana likely end with much more?
- A) Ben's contributions were taxed more.
- B) She paid lower fees.
- C) She chose riskier investments.
- D) Her money had 10 more years to compound. ✅

**Explanation:** starting early gives compounding more time, which matters more than the extra amount paid in. *(3.2)*

### Q4. A 24-year-old has high-interest credit card debt and no savings. What is usually the best first step?
- A) Open a 10-year locked-term instrument.
- B) Pay off the expensive debt and start an emergency fund. ✅
- C) Invest everything in a stock index fund.
- D) Buy a speculative asset to earn money quickly.

**Explanation:** expensive debt often costs more than investments earn, and a safety net protects the plan. *(3.2)*

### Q5. Sam, 40, has an emergency fund, a renovation in 2 years and retirement in 25 years. What's the best way to organize this?
- A) Everything in cash until retirement
- B) One aggressive allocation for all goals
- C) Pause retirement saving until the renovation is done, then invest everything in stocks
- D) A separate allocation per goal: cash, a steady mix for the renovation, growth for retirement ✅

**Explanation:** each goal has its own horizon, so each deserves its own level of risk. *(3.3)*

### Q6. What is a glide path?
- A) A plan that gradually reduces risk as the target date approaches ✅
- B) Increasing risk to catch up before retirement
- C) A type of pension account
- D) Switching everything to cash on the day you retire

**Explanation:** a glide path lowers risk step by step, rather than all at once. *(3.4)*

### Q7. A 30% market crash happens right before Eva retires and starts withdrawing money. What is this risk called?
- A) Liquidity risk
- B) Credit risk
- C) Sequence risk ✅
- D) Concentration risk

**Explanation:** losses right before or early in retirement hurt most, because withdrawals lock them in. *(3.4)*

### Q8. Leo expects to need 2,800 coins a month in retirement and to receive a 1,600 coin public pension. What is his monthly retirement gap?
- A) 600 coins
- B) 1,200 coins ✅
- C) 1,600 coins
- D) 4,400 coins

**Explanation:** 2,800 − 1,600 = 1,200 coins a month to cover from his own savings. *(3.5)*

### Q9. What is the usual trade-off of a tax-advantaged personal pension account?
- A) Tax benefits in exchange for keeping the money locked until a certain age ✅
- B) There is no trade-off.
- C) Higher fees in exchange for guaranteed returns
- D) Free insurance in exchange for no investment choice

**Explanation:** tax advantages usually come with lock-up rules and penalties for early withdrawal. *(3.6)*

### Q10. Your target is 70% market basket / 30% bonds. After a strong year you hold 80% / 20%. What does rebalancing mean here?
- A) Selling everything and holding cash
- B) Doing nothing, since the portfolio grew
- C) Buying more of the basket because it's doing well
- D) Moving money from the basket to bonds (or directing new money to bonds) to return to 70/30 ✅

**Explanation:** rebalancing restores your intended level of risk after the market makes the portfolio drift. *(3.7)*
