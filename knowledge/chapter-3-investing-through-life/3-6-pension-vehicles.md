# 3.6 Pension Vehicles

**Chapter:** 3 — Investing Through Life
**Goal of this subchapter:** the learner can recognize the main types of retirement accounts and their typical features: tax advantages, employer contributions and lock-up rules.

> This lesson is generic. Exact account types, limits and tax rules differ by country. If localized later, keep the content educational and don't recommend specific products.

---

## Lesson

### The three pillars

Many pension systems are described as three "pillars":

| Pillar | What it is | Who pays in |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Public pension** | The state pension system | Mandatory contributions from workers and employers |
| **2. Employer schemes** | Workplace pension plans | Often both the employee and the employer |
| **3. Personal retirement accounts** | Accounts you open yourself | You |

### Employer schemes

- Contributions are often taken **automatically from your salary**.
- Many employers **add their own money** on top. That's part of your pay: skipping it can mean leaving free money behind.
- Some countries enroll workers **automatically**, and you have to opt out actively.

### Personal retirement accounts

- You choose how much to put in, often within a **yearly limit**.
- Inside the account you can usually choose **instruments**, such as funds, bonds and deposits.
- They often come with **tax advantages**.

### Typical tax advantages

Depending on the account and the country, tax benefits may include:

- **Tax relief on contributions:** what you pay in lowers your taxable income today.
- **Tax-free growth:** no tax on gains while the money stays in the account.
- **Lower or no tax on withdrawal**, if the rules are followed.

### The trade-off: lock-up rules

Tax advantages usually come with **conditions**:

- Money is meant to stay in until a **minimum age**.
- Early withdrawals may mean **losing the tax benefit** or paying a **penalty**.

That makes pension accounts **low-liquidity** instruments, similar to the Locked-term instrument in the sandbox. That's why the **emergency fund stays outside** the pension account.

### Key takeaways

- Retirement income often comes from three pillars: public, employer and personal.
- Employer contributions are effectively extra pay.
- Personal pension accounts often offer tax advantages.
- In exchange, the money is usually locked until retirement age.

## Control questions

### Q1. What are the three pillars of a typical pension system?
- A) Savings, spending and debt
- B) Cash, stocks and bonds
- C) Public pension, employer schemes and personal retirement accounts ✅
- D) Young, middle-aged and retired

**Explanation:** retirement income usually combines these three sources.

### Q2. Your employer offers to add money to your workplace pension if you contribute too. What does skipping it usually mean?
- A) Leaving part of your pay unused ✅
- B) Avoiding all risk
- C) Nothing important
- D) Getting a higher salary instead

**Explanation:** employer contributions are effectively extra pay you only get by taking part.

### Q3. What is a common trade-off of tax-advantaged pension accounts?
- A) They can only hold cash.
- B) They have no fees.
- C) They're only available to the rich.
- D) The money is usually locked until a certain age, with penalties for early withdrawal. ✅

**Explanation:** tax benefits are given in return for keeping the money invested for retirement.

### Q4. Why should your emergency fund not be kept inside a pension account?
- A) Emergency funds should be invested in stocks.
- B) The money may not be accessible without penalties when an emergency happens. ✅
- C) Pension accounts are too safe.
- D) Pension accounts don't allow cash.

**Explanation:** an emergency fund needs high liquidity, and pension accounts have low liquidity.

### Q5. Which is a possible tax advantage of a pension account?
- A) No need to ever withdraw
- B) Free insurance
- C) Guaranteed returns
- D) Gains growing without being taxed while the money stays in the account ✅

**Explanation:** tax-free or tax-deferred growth is a common benefit.
